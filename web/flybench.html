Experiment 1 · probe ladder

Why olfactory learning fails

The working theory blamed overlapping Kenyon-cell codes. Three increasingly constrained readouts test that directly.

Theory refuted

Each rung adds one biological constraint and asks the same question: can the two odours be told apart from Kenyon-cell activity? The rung where accuracy collapses is the thing to fix.

None of them collapse. Even the most constrained readout — the real KC→MBON wiring, weights allowed only to decrease, the exact expressive range of the dopamine rule — separates the odours 93.3% of the time on held-out trials.

Depression-only learning could shift discrimination by +0.217. The real rule delivers −0.040, in the opposite direction.

That is a factor of five and a sign error. The codes are separable, the anatomy can express the discrimination, and depression is a sufficient mechanism. Kenyon-cell overlap is not the blocker — and it was measured at 72.2% here, worse than the 36.1% the theory was built on, while the ceiling stayed high anyway.

The failure is in credit assignment: which synapses the dopamine signal reaches. Narrowing the odour codes would not have helped.

Experiment 2 · sensory drive

The optic lobe is switched off

58% of the connectome is visual. What reaches the rest of the brain when you turn the lights on?

Zero output

Driving all 4,107 photoreceptors at 400 Hz produces 224,794 receptor spikes and exactly zero spikes in the visual projection neurons, the descending bus, or anywhere downstream. Not few. Zero.

The cause is in the data, not the code. Every visual receptor is histaminergic, and all 29,469 of their outgoing edges carry sign −1: in the fly, photoreceptor output is inhibitory. Combine that with the model's 0 Hz resting rate — correct, and published — and the visual system is a switch wired to nothing. There is no activity for it to suppress.

Vision cannot work in a network with no baseline, because its first synapse is inhibitory.

Mechanosensory drive at less than half the rate produces 10,324 descending spikes through the same machinery, which is what a working sensory channel looks like. The fix is not more drive; it is a tonic baseline for the visual system to modulate downward.

Experiment 3 · escape habituation

It sensitises instead

Twenty identical harmless stimuli, then a novel one. A habituating circuit responds less each time.

No habituation

The escape pathway does the opposite of habituating. Over twenty repetitions the descending population climbs to 1.52× its first response, and the novel stimulus is treated identically — so nothing here is stimulus-specific.

This was predicted before running. There is no plastic synapse anywhere in this pathway, no synaptic depression, and no spike-frequency adaptation, so residual excitation has nowhere to go but up.

The Giant Fiber fires about one spike per pulse — too sparse to read, the same problem that sank the single-neuron steering readout.

Habituation is the canonical learning paradigm in both zebrafish and C. elegans, and it needs no mushroom body. Getting it requires adding short-term synaptic depression — a mechanism the model currently lacks entirely.

Experiment 4 · embodied episode

It starved with food nearby

The creature above, given seven food sources and 189 ticks to find one.

Starved

Zero feeding events. Zero drinking events. Energy and water fall in straight lines from full to empty, because nothing ever interrupted them, and the episode ends at tick 188 with died:starved.

The steering signal itself is real and was confirmed separately — the DNa family discriminates a left from a right stimulus at d′=4.21, p=3e−11. But across the whole episode the turn command stays positive on 8 of 10 sampled ticks and speed never leaves the 0.20–0.23 band.

A real steering signal, a body that can move, and no behaviour connecting them.

This is the honest state of the creature: senses work, the action bus works, and chemotaxis does not close the loop. It is worth saying plainly, because a foraging demo is exactly the kind of thing that is easy to fake and hard to falsify.

Reading this

Three of four experiments returned negative results. Each was designed so a negative result names the next thing to fix, which is the only reason to run an experiment you expect to fail.
